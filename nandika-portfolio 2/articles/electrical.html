Touch Voltage and Leakage-Current Exposure in Wired Audio Systems

What a childhood shock at a microphone taught me about protective earthing, fault paths, and how little a single voltage reading can say.

Research and technical article

The night I was lifted off my feet

I was five, at a community talent show, singing into a wired microphone. The next thing I knew, I was on the floor and the back of my skull was on concrete.

I walked away with no injuries. That is the part that has bothered me ever since. I was a small kid who had been knocked flat by something invisible and landed hard, and nothing was wrong with me. I wanted an explanation, and over time that question turned into this research.

I want to be careful about what this page is. It is an investigation motivated by that event, not a forensic reconstruction of it. I never saw the equipment again, no one measured the circuit, and I was five. What I can do is work out which physical mechanisms could put a current through a performer at a microphone, which ones the numbers rule out, and what protective systems exist to stop it happening.

What I know and what I don’t

OBSERVED

A wired microphone, a stage performance, a sudden loss of footing, a fall onto concrete, and no injury afterwards.

NOT KNOWN

The sound system, its wiring and earthing, the power source, the current path through my body, the duration, and whether the fall came from muscle contraction or loss of consciousness.

SO THE QUESTION IS

Which mechanism classes are physically capable of producing this, and which are not?

Candidate mechanisms

A microphone is held against the mouth and hands, so the body can become a path between the microphone’s metal parts and whatever the performer touches or stands on. Broadly, there are four ways a hazardous potential can appear there:

  1. A mains fault reaching exposed metal. Damaged insulation, a pinched cable or an internal fault can connect a live conductor to a chassis or shield. Protective earth exists to carry that fault current and trip a protective device. If the earth connection is missing or broken, the metal stays energized.
  2. Miswiring or a defeated earth. Reversed polarity, an adaptor that removes the earth pin, or an earth lifted to cure hum all remove the intended protection while the equipment appears to work normally.
  3. Potential difference between interconnected equipment. Amplifier, mixer and stage can sit at different potentials, and the signal cable’s shield can then carry current into the microphone.
  4. Capacitive leakage. Mains filters deliberately couple a small capacitance to the chassis, which can raise a floating metal part to a measurable voltage.

I cannot say which of these happened. The useful question is how much current each one can deliver, which is a number I can compute.

Modeling capacitive leakage

For an ordinary mains-derived leakage path, the body acts as a resistor R in series with the coupling capacitor C. The capacitor’s reactance is XC = 1/(2πfC), and the current magnitude is I = V / √(R² + XC²). Change any value below to see where it lands against approximate physiological thresholds for 50/60 Hz current from IEC/TS 60479-1. The thresholds are not fixed numbers: IEC 60479-1 shows them depending strongly on how long the current flows, as well as on the person, the path and the contact, and its body data come largely from adults, so for a child the result is more uncertain.

The default case, a 2.2 nF filter capacitor of the size commonly used for this purpose, passes about a tenth of a milliamp: well below perception. To reach a shock that could knock a person over, a purely capacitive path would need hundreds of nanofarads, far beyond anything designed into audio equipment. My inference is that an ordinary leakage path is very unlikely to explain what happened to me. A conductive fault, such as a live conductor touching exposed metal with no working earth, is the more plausible class. That is a ranking of likelihoods from physics, not a finding about that stage.

Why a fall, and why no injury

Three findings from the literature frame this, and they pull in different directions.

  1. A shock can cause the fall. Currents above the let-go threshold cause involuntary muscle contraction, and the secondary effects, falling and hitting the head, are recognized as a hazard in themselves in biomedical-engineering teaching notes on electrical safety. So the head strike on concrete need not have been an electrical effect.
  2. Duration changes the thresholds a great deal. A summary of IEC TS 60479-1 gives a let-go threshold from about 5 mA for contact longer than 6 seconds to about 200 mA at 10 milliseconds, and ventricular fibrillation risk from roughly 40 mA once flow exceeds about 2 seconds. A very brief contact, for example one broken by being thrown clear, can therefore carry a large current without lasting injury. That is a hypothesis consistent with my outcome, not something I can show.
  3. Smaller bodies have less margin, not more. In animal studies the fibrillation threshold rises with body weight (one set of lecture notes quotes 50 mA for 6 kg dogs against 130 mA for 24 kg dogs), so a five-year-old plausibly had a lower threshold than an adult.

I therefore treat the lack of injury as one outcome that needs no special explanation beyond brevity and luck, and not as evidence that such an event is safe. A head strike on concrete after an electrical event deserves medical evaluation whatever the person feels.

What the protective systems are for

Each protective measure does a different job. Protective-earth bonding gives a fault current a low-impedance path so that an overcurrent device opens quickly. A ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI, or residual-current device elsewhere) compares outgoing and returning current and trips on a small imbalance, which is the layer that protects a person rather than the wiring. Double insulation removes the need for an earth by construction. No one layer substitutes for another, which is why lifting an earth to silence hum is dangerous.

Practically, my conclusion is modest: performance equipment should be inspected by a qualified person, protected by a GFCI on the supply, and never modified to defeat its earth. This page does not describe how to test energized equipment, and no one should probe a suspect system to see whether it is live.
