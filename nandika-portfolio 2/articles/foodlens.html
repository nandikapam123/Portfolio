FoodLens: Using Technology to Make Food Resources Findable

A community app, the scale of the problem it addresses, and the requirements and evaluation I hold it to.

Repository ↗ Technovation Girls U.S. Top 15 semifinalist

The problem, with numbers

The USDA’s Economic Research Service reports that 13.5% of U.S. households (18.0 million) were food insecure at some time in 2023, up from 12.8% in 2022, and that 17.9% of households with children were affected. Texas had one of the highest state rates; a news summary of the same USDA report puts it at 16.9% for 2023. Food insecurity is not the same as hunger, and the USDA definition is about uncertain or limited access to enough food because of limited resources, which is exactly a problem of finding and reaching the right resource.

What FoodLens is

FoodLens is a community app, built to help people find food resources and navigate dietary needs (repository). It was named a Technovation Girls Challenge U.S. Top 15 semifinalist, and local teens’ work on it was covered by KXAN. I treat the app as one part of a response, not a solution to structural hunger.

Design requirements

RequirementWhyHow I check it
FindabilityThe problem is access to information, not only to foodTask test: a new user finds a suitable resource; record time and errors
Fit to dietary needsAllergies and restrictions make generic advice unusableTest with allergen and restriction cases; wrong matches count as failures
Accuracy over timeA listing that has closed is worse than no listingRecord a last-verified date on each resource and flag stale ones
AccessibilityUsers may be on old phones, stressed, or reading in a second languageText contrast of at least 4.5:1 (WCAG 2.2 AA), plain-language wording
PrivacyUsers should not have to disclose income or health to get helpCollect no personal data that a feature does not need
No false authorityAn app cannot give medical adviceDietary information is labelled as general, not clinical

How I would evaluate it

Success measures are task completion rate, time to find a suitable resource, and the System Usability Scale (SUS), which scores from 0 to 100, with about 68 as the conventional average. Because the users are the community, evaluation should be done with their consent and with small, honest samples that report the number of participants. I do not claim user counts or outcomes for the app here.
