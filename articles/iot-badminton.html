Badminton Swing Sensor

A developing hardware-and-software project to turn physical swing signals into useful, interpretable information for a player preparing for competition.

UNDER DEVELOPMENTResearch in progress

The idea

Badminton performance depends on more than equipment: movement timing, repeatability, and how a player feels before a tournament can all matter. I am working on an electronic IoT device intended to collect data during badminton swings, paired with an ML program that can analyze the physical signals and surface patterns for review.

The goal is to investigate whether repeated, measured swings can provide useful context—not to make medical claims or promise a match outcome.

System architecture

Sensor layer

Capture motion-related signals from a device mounted in a consistent position. Sensor choice, sample rate, range, and calibration remain design decisions to validate.

Signal processing

Clean noise, align time windows, segment swing events, and derive features that can be compared across repetitions.

Machine learning

Compare a simple baseline with candidate models using separated training and test sessions. Avoid letting near-identical swings leak across the split.

Player-facing summary

Translate results into understandable trends with confidence and limitations visible, so athletes can reflect on patterns rather than blindly trust a score.

What the pipeline does, step by step

The trace above is generated as five Gaussian acceleration pulses (about 30 ms wide, 9–10 g) riding on 1 g of gravity and slow body motion, sampled at 400 Hz with 0.6 g of Gaussian noise. That sample rate puts the Nyquist limit at 200 Hz, comfortably above the roughly 10–20 Hz content of a swing pulse, so the filter can discard high-frequency noise without distorting the pulse. The filter is a first-order low-pass, y[k] = y[k−1] + α(x[k] − y[k−1]) with α = Δt / (RC + Δt) and a 30 Hz cutoff. Events are then found with a 5 g threshold: each excursion above it yields one peak, which stops one swing being counted twice.

From each event I extract peak magnitude and the interval to the previous swing. The coefficient of variation of peak magnitude is the first repeatability number I would want from a real session: a small CV says the sensor and the player are consistent, a large one says the mounting, the sensor range or the player is not. For the model stage, the validation rule that matters most is to split by session, not by swing. Swings recorded minutes apart are nearly identical, so a random split leaks them across train and test and inflates accuracy.

What prior work says, and what I take from it

StudySetupReported result
Ooi & Gouwanda (Proc. IMechE Part P 237, 2023)Wireless inertial sensors and a neural network, stroke identification97.69% accuracy with several sensors; 95.09% with a single wrist sensor
BadminSense (arXiv 2603.21825)Single smartwatch IMU; 12 experienced amateurs; SVM91.43% stroke classification when tested on players unseen in training (leave-3-users-out)
Lin et al. 2020, as summarized in a Mekatronika reviewIMU plus acoustic sensor in the racketHit detection above 99.9%; stroke classification 96.5% for personalized models and a lower figure (84%) otherwise
Tennis stroke classification (TU Delft MSc thesis)Single forearm IMU; SVM; leave-one-out validationWorks, but small data and mixed skill levels weaken confidence in the results

Three decisions follow. First, the evaluation protocol drives the headline number: the gap between personalized and general models (96.5% versus 84% in one summary) and BadminSense’s 91% on unseen players show that random splits flatter a model, so mine will be split by session and by player. Second, one wrist sensor comes close to many sensors, so the hardware stays minimal. Third, hit detection is largely solved, so what I add should not be stroke labelling; consistency metrics across repeated swings are a less-covered question.

Research questions

  • Can the device capture repeated swings consistently across sessions?
  • Which features are stable enough to compare—such as event timing or variation between repetitions?
  • Does a model generalize to a different day or player, or does it only recognize its training data?
  • Can the results be explained clearly enough to support athlete reflection?

Validation and responsible use

Before interpreting player status, the device needs repeatability tests, a documented data-collection protocol, and a clear definition of the target outcome. Data should be collected with informed consent, stored securely, and limited to what the project needs. Model outputs should include uncertainty and should not be framed as a medical assessment or a definitive measure of readiness.

This project is under development and research. The sensors, data features, model performance, and player-facing outputs have not been asserted as validated results.
