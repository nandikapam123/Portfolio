STRIKE: A Racket Finder Built as a Transparent Decision Model

Weighted multi-criteria scoring with sensitivity checks, plus a physical argument for treating balance point as a measured quantity.

Repository ↗ IoT + ML research in development

The problem I saw

I play badminton competitively and represent my local club at tournaments around the United States. Players choose rackets from long specification sheets and conflicting advice. STRIKE (repository) is an interactive tool that narrows that choice. This entry sets out the decision model I hold it to: one a player can inspect and re-weight, not a single opaque “best racket” score.

Decision model

Each racket is a vector of attributes (weight, balance point, shaft flexibility, head shape, price). The model is a weighted-sum multi-criteria score: normalize every attribute to [0, 1] (min–max, with the direction flipped where lower is better), multiply by the player’s weights, and rank. Three checks keep it honest. Missing values are kept as missing, not as zeros. Units are normalized before comparison. Sensitivity: perturb each weight by ±20% and report how often the top choice changes, since a recommendation that flips under small changes in preference should be shown as a tie, not a winner.

Manufacturer descriptions such as “head-heavy” or “fast” are labels, not measurements. Where possible the model uses quantities with units, and the next section shows why one of them, balance point, matters more than its small numeric range suggests.

Why balance point deserves a weight

A racket’s resistance to being swung is its moment of inertia about the grip. Modelling it as a handle-and-shaft mass and a head mass (assumed at 0.20 m and 0.56 m from the butt) with a fixed 85 g total, moving the balance point changes the inertia substantially for a small change in position.

The takeaway for the tool is that balance point should be a first-class attribute with units (mm from the butt), not a three-way label, and that players comparing two rackets should compare swing inertia, not weight alone. For the rule limits on racket size, see the Badminton World Federation laws.

The next layer: measuring the swing

The racket finder uses specifications. The sensing extension would add measured movement from the player, in the entry on the Badminton Swing Sensor, which also covers the prior work and the validation plan. I treat it as research in progress; no performance claims are made for it.
